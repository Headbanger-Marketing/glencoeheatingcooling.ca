AC Repair

Does the indoor fan run, and is the cooling problem affecting every room or only one area?

An air conditioner that runs without cooling can have an electrical fault, an airflow problem, or an issue in its refrigerant circuit. The diagnosis differs between a central system with ductwork and a ductless unit serving a particular room or addition. Record when cooling stopped, whether the indoor fan operates, and any visible water or error indication. These observations help narrow the assessment, while inspection findings determine whether a component repair or a larger system decision is warranted.

A useful assessment compares thermostat demand, indoor airflow, outdoor-unit operation, and any fault codes, while identifying whether the equipment is central or ductless. This sequence helps separate a control or electrical fault from an airflow or refrigerant-circuit problem. The system’s repair history and the condition of major components also matter when weighing a targeted repair against replacement.

Assessment and Scope

Questions for the Proposed Work

01

Check the electrical and control sequence

A capacitor, contactor, wiring connection, or control fault can prevent an outdoor unit from starting or cause it to stop unexpectedly. Diagnosis should establish whether the equipment receives the correct call and how it responds, rather than assuming the whole condenser has failed. Capacitors and internal electrical parts are not homeowner repair tasks. The specific failed component, its condition, and any related damage determine whether a focused repair is appropriate.

02

Distinguish airflow issues from refrigerant faults

Weak airflow may point toward a blower, filter, coil, or duct issue, while poor cooling with normal airflow calls for further system checks. A refrigerant-circuit concern requires professional assessment and should not be addressed by adding refrigerant without identifying the cause. For a ductless system, compare operation across indoor heads because one zone may behave differently from another. The location and pattern of the symptom help direct the inspection.

03

Compare repair with replacement on evidence

A first failure involving a repairable electrical component may justify repair if the compressor, coil, and other major parts remain in suitable condition. Repeated breakdowns, a major compressor or coil fault, or unavailable parts can change that decision. Compare the confirmed fault, repair history, equipment condition, and compatibility of replacement options rather than using breakdown count alone. A diagnosis should explain why a repair is likely to address the fault or why replacement merits consideration.

Compare the next step

Identify whether each earlier repair addressed the same diagnosed cause or a different symptom. Ask which measurements support the next recommendation and how the completed repair will be verified. Compare the complete fault history with a replacement proposal before treating repeated visits as proof that replacement is unavoidable.
